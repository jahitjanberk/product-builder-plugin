# Customer discovery interviews

Load this when planning, running, or synthesizing problem-discovery interviews. The goal is to learn whether the problem is real, how people handle it today, and what it costs them. The idea itself is never what gets validated. These rules follow the approach popularized by Rob Fitzpatrick's *The Mom Test*: even people who want to be nice to you can't mislead you if you only ask about their life, not your idea.

## Rules

- **Talk about their life, not your idea.** Leave the product out of the interview entirely, or at least until the last few minutes. Once it's described, people evaluate it politely instead of reporting what they actually do.
- **Ask about specific past events, not generalities or the future.** "Tell me about the last time X happened" gets a real story. "How often do you…" gets an idealized average. "Would you…" gets fiction.
- **Listen far more than you talk.** Aim for the interviewee speaking at least 80% of the time. Silence after an answer often produces the most useful detail.
- **Dig under every feature request and emotion.** "Why do you want that?" "What would that let you do?" "What happened the last time that came up?" Requests are symptoms; the job is underneath.
- **Look for what they've already spent.** Time, money, or effort already invested in a workaround is the strongest sign the problem is real. No workaround usually means the pain is mild.

## Questions

**Avoid:**
- "Would you use a product that…?"
- "How much would you pay for…?"
- "Do you think this is a good idea?"
- "Would it be useful if…?"
- Any question that contains your solution

**Use:**
- "Walk me through the last time you had to [job]."
- "What did you do about it? What else have you tried?"
- "What was the hardest part? Why?"
- "What does that cost you, in time, money, or hassle?"
- "Who else is involved when this happens?"
- "What are you using now? What do you like and dislike about it?"
- "Have you looked for a better way? What happened?"
- "Is there anything else I should have asked?"

## Script (30 minutes)

1. **Intro (2 min):** "I'm researching how people handle [job area]. I'm not selling anything, and there are no wrong answers. Can I take notes?"
2. **Context (5 min):** their role and setup, and how [job area] fits into their week.
3. **Last-time story (10 min):** "Tell me about the last time you [job]." Follow the story step by step: trigger, actions, tools, people, outcome.
4. **Current alternative and cost (6 min):** what they use today, what it costs, and what they've tried and abandoned.
5. **Switching (4 min):** "What would have to change for you to try something different?" "When did you last switch tools for this? What triggered it?"
6. **Advancement ask (3 min):** ask for something that costs them a little:
   - a follow-up session
   - an intro to a colleague who has the same problem
   - trying an early version
   - a pre-order or letter of intent

   How they respond is the most important data point of the interview.

## Signals

| What you hear | Type | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| "That's a great idea!" / "I'd love that" | Compliment | Ignore it. Steer back to past behavior. |
| "I usually…", "I always…", "I would…" | Fluff (generic or hypothetical) | Anchor it: "When was the last time? Walk me through it." |
| "It would be great if it also did X" | Feature request | Ask why. Record the underlying job, not the feature. |
| "We built a spreadsheet / hired someone / pay for Y to handle this" | Evidence of spend | Strong signal. Record the cost. |
| Agrees to a follow-up, intro, pilot, or pre-order | Commitment / advancement | The real validation. |
| "Sounds interesting, keep me posted" | Polite rejection | Treat it as a no. |

## Recruiting and sample size

- Recruit from the PRD's target segment only. Interviews with people outside the segment produce confident, irrelevant data.
- Plan **5–8 interviews per segment**. Stop when two or three interviews in a row add no new themes. If themes keep diverging, the segment is probably two segments.
- Don't interview only friends, existing fans, or people who replied enthusiastically to a launch post. They skew toward people who already like you.

## Synthesis

1. **Write notes within an hour** of each interview. Use verbatim quotes for anything important, and separate observed facts from your interpretation.
2. **Tag each note** with one of: job, context, current alternative, cost/spend, switching trigger, pain, or commitment signal.
3. **Cluster across interviews** (an affinity map). For each theme, record how many interviewees showed it and the strongest evidence type (spend > specific story > generic statement > compliment).
4. **Weigh evidence, not volume.** One interviewee who pays for a workaround outweighs five who said it "sounds useful". Don't let a single articulate interviewee set the direction.
5. **Update the PRD.**
   - Audience/JTBD table: the job, context, current alternative, switching trigger, and who to exclude, with quotes as evidence.
   - Problem section: add the evidence.
   - Open questions: record anything that's still unvalidated.
6. **State the conclusion plainly**: validated, invalidated, or inconclusive, plus what would change it. "Inconclusive" is a legitimate result, and it means more discovery, not building anyway.
